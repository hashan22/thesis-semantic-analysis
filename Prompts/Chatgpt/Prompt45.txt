Several critical warning signs were missed by Austrian authorities in the lead-up to the November 2, 2020 terrorist attack in Vienna, perpetrated by Kujtim Fejzulai, a 20-year-old radicalized Austrian-Macedonian dual national. These oversights—despite his criminal record, extremist ties, and suspicious behavior—demonstrated systemic failures in Austria’s intelligence, probation, and judicial systems.
Here are the main missed warning signs:
________________________________________
1. Attempted Purchase of Ammunition in Slovakia (July 2020)
Fejzulai traveled to Slovakia in July 2020 to buy ammunition for an AK-47 assault rifle.
•	He lacked a gun permit, and the purchase was refused.
•	Slovak police notified Austrian authorities immediately.
•	However, no significant follow-up occurred within Austria’s domestic intelligence service (BVT).
🛑 Missed Opportunity: This was a clear red flag — a parolee convicted of terrorism attempting to buy ammunition abroad. It should have triggered surveillance, a parole review, or re-arrest.
________________________________________
2. Post-Release Behavior & Increasing Radicalization
Fejzulai was released from prison in December 2019 after serving two-thirds of a 22-month sentence for attempting to join ISIS.
•	He was under parole conditions until December 2022.
•	According to reports, he became increasingly religiously extreme in the months leading up to the attack.
•	His de-radicalization supervisor from DERAD flagged these changes, but authorities did not act.
🛑 Missed Opportunity: A visible re-escalation in extremist beliefs should have led to enhanced monitoring, especially given his known history and parole status.
________________________________________
3. Online Activity Before the Attack
Hours before the attack, Fejzulai posted a photo of himself on Instagram posing with weapons and a declaration of loyalty to ISIS.
•	He wore a fake suicide vest, held an automatic weapon, and displayed jihadi symbolism.
🛑 Missed Opportunity: Authorities failed to monitor his social media effectively — something that should have been part of post-release supervision.
________________________________________
4. Meeting with Known Extremists (July 2020)
Fejzulai attended a meeting in Vienna with Islamist extremists from Germany and Switzerland — individuals already under surveillance in their home countries.
•	Two of them were later arrested by Swiss authorities.
•	This event was known to Austrian agencies, but again no significant action was taken.
🛑 Missed Opportunity: Cross-national extremist gatherings are typically high-priority alerts — his attendance should have flagged him as actively networked and dangerous.
________________________________________
5. Flawed Risk Assessment (RADAR-iTE)
Austria uses the RADAR-iTE tool to assess the threat level of individuals convicted of terrorist offenses.
•	Fejzulai's risk assessment was delayed, and he was not classified as high risk in time.
•	This delayed or weakened preventive actions like house searches, surveillance, or re-arrest.
🛑 Missed Opportunity: The delay in applying this tool contributed to underestimating his threat level.
________________________________________
6. Lack of Real-Time Intelligence Sharing
Information from DERAD, BVT, LVT Wien, and international agencies was not effectively shared or centralized.
•	No single agency had a complete view of his activities.
•	Fragmentation prevented a coordinated preventive response.
🛑 Missed Opportunity: There was no integrated case management system for high-risk individuals.

📌 Conclusion
The Vienna attack was not a sudden or unforeseeable act. Fejzulai had multiple interactions with state institutions—judicial, correctional, and intelligence—each offering a chance to intervene. Unfortunately, fragmented communication, a lack of real-time oversight, and underestimation of his post-release threat level contributed to a tragedy that many experts now describe as preventable. The case became a catalyst for reforms in Austria’s counterterrorism framework.
