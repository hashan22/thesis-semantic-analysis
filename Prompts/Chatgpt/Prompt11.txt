As a Member of Austria’s Muslim Community:
In the days following the Vienna attack on November 2, 2020, I felt a deep and painful mix of sorrow, fear, and responsibility — not because I or my community were involved, but because once again, an act of terror carried out by one individual risked casting a shadow over millions of peaceful Muslims who call Austria home.
We were mourning like everyone else. We cried for the victims. We felt the pain of our city being attacked. But alongside the grief, there was also a silent worry: Would the public see us as part of the problem or part of the solution?
The media response was, at times, balanced — highlighting that this attacker did not represent Islam. But there were also moments where headlines, commentary, or public discourse veered into generalizations, lumping all Muslims into a narrative of suspicion. That was deeply hurtful.
What many didn’t see were the dozens of Muslim organizations that immediately condemned the attack, the mosques that held vigils, and the imams who reminded their congregations that violence is forbidden in our faith. We were not silent — we were grieving and speaking out, just not always heard.
At the same time, I also felt hopeful. Many Austrians — friends, colleagues, neighbors — reached out to us in solidarity. They reminded us that we belong here, that we are part of the shared sorrow and healing. It is those moments of connection that I hold onto.
We, as Austrian Muslims, want nothing more than peace and safety in our home. We stand against extremism, and we also ask to be seen not as suspects, but as fellow citizens committed to building a more united, compassionate society.
